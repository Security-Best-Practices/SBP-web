Ransomware in 2025: What Every Business Needs to Know

By Keith W. Salustro, CISSP® — March 2025

Ransomware isn't new. What's new is how professional it has become. The loosely organized criminal groups that plagued businesses in 2019 have been replaced by highly structured ransomware-as-a-service (RaaS) operations that function like legitimate software businesses — complete with support teams, affiliate programs, and negotiation portals.

If you haven't updated your understanding of ransomware since 2022 or 2023, you may be defending against a threat that no longer exists in the form you expect — while the real threat walks right through your front door.

What Has Changed in Ransomware Since 2023

Four major shifts define the current ransomware landscape:

Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) is now the dominant model. Developers build and maintain the ransomware platform. Affiliates — criminals who may have no technical skills of their own — pay a subscription or revenue share to deploy attacks. This has dramatically lowered the barrier to entry and increased attack volume.

Double extortion is standard practice. Attackers no longer just encrypt your data and demand payment to unlock it. They also exfiltrate the data first and threaten to publish it publicly if you don't pay. For businesses in healthcare, legal, or financial services, that second threat — the public exposure of client data — can be more damaging than the encryption itself.

Initial access brokers have professionalized the attack chain. A separate underground market has emerged for "initial access" — meaning someone else has already broken into your network and is selling that foothold to ransomware affiliates. By the time ransomware deploys, an attacker may have been inside your network for weeks or months.

Dwell time has shrunk dramatically. In 2020, attackers often spent 3–4 weeks inside a network before deploying ransomware, giving defenders time to detect the intrusion. By 2024, median dwell time had dropped to under 24 hours in many campaigns. You may have very little time between compromise and encryption.

Who Is Being Targeted — And Why SMBs Are at Higher Risk

Large enterprises still get hit, but mid-size and small businesses are disproportionately targeted for a simple reason: they tend to have valuable data and weak defenses. A 200-person professional services firm holds client financial data, health records, or privileged legal communications — the same categories of sensitive data as a large corporation — but typically lacks the security team or tools to defend it.

Ransomware groups have also learned that mid-market companies are more likely to pay. Large enterprises often have cyber insurance, legal teams, and hardened backup environments. Small businesses are more likely to be desperate enough to pay quickly just to get operations back online.

Certain industries face elevated risk: healthcare (HIPAA-regulated data), legal (privilege and client confidentiality), financial services (PII and account data), and any business in a supply chain with large enterprise customers, since attackers can use you as a stepping stone to reach a larger target.

The Five Vectors Attackers Use to Get In

Understanding how ransomware enters your environment is the first step to stopping it. The most common entry points in 2024 and 2025:

  1. Phishing emails. Still the number-one vector. Attackers have gotten dramatically better at crafting convincing emails — using AI to eliminate grammatical errors, impersonating vendors your employees actually work with, and timing attacks around real business events like invoicing periods or M&A activity.
  2. Exposed RDP and remote access services. Remote Desktop Protocol (RDP) ports that are internet-accessible are a constant target. If your organization still has RDP open to the internet — or VPN services running outdated firmware — you are at elevated risk.
  3. Compromised credentials. Stolen username/password combinations purchased on the dark web, or obtained through prior phishing, allow attackers to log in as legitimate users. Without multi-factor authentication (MFA), there's nothing stopping them.
  4. Unpatched software. Known vulnerabilities in edge devices — firewalls, VPNs, email gateways — are actively exploited within days or even hours of a CVE being published. Slow patching is one of the most common ways organizations get compromised.
  5. Supply chain and managed service providers (MSPs). Attackers increasingly target MSPs and software vendors because a single compromise can provide access to dozens or hundreds of downstream clients simultaneously.

What Good Ransomware Defense Actually Looks Like

Effective ransomware defense isn't a single product. It's a set of overlapping controls that make an attack progressively harder at every stage.

Multi-factor authentication everywhere. MFA on email, VPN, remote access, and cloud services is the single highest-impact control you can implement. It won't stop every attack, but it blocks the large majority of credential-based intrusions. There is no acceptable reason not to have MFA on your email and remote access systems in 2025.

Network segmentation. If your entire network is flat — meaning any device can talk to any other device — ransomware that gets onto one machine will spread rapidly to all of them. Segmenting your network (separating workstations from servers, finance from operations, etc.) limits the blast radius of an attack.

Immutable, tested backups. "Air-gapped" or immutable backups that attackers cannot access or encrypt are your recovery option of last resort. Three critical words: tested, offsite, and immutable. A backup that has never been restored is not a backup. Backups stored on the same network the attacker controls are not a backup.

Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR). Traditional antivirus is no longer sufficient. EDR solutions monitor endpoint behavior in real time and can detect and respond to ransomware activity — file encryption patterns, lateral movement, unusual process behavior — before it completes. If you are still running only legacy antivirus, you have a significant gap.

Patch management discipline. Critical patches — especially for internet-facing systems and edge devices — should be applied within days of release, not weeks or months. Establish a patching cadence and enforce it.

Incident response planning. When ransomware hits, organizations without a documented incident response plan lose hours or days figuring out who to call, what systems to isolate, and what their legal notification obligations are. That time costs money and compounds the damage. An IR plan doesn't need to be 200 pages — it needs to be clear, current, and rehearsed.

30-Day Ransomware Risk Reduction Checklist

You don't need to solve every problem at once. These actions, taken within 30 days, will materially reduce your ransomware risk:

  1. Enable MFA on all email accounts and remote access (VPN, RDP) — this week.
  2. Audit who has RDP or other remote access open to the internet. Close or restrict any that don't need to be exposed.
  3. Verify you have immutable backups in place and test restoration of at least one critical system.
  4. Apply any outstanding critical patches on firewalls, VPNs, and email gateways.
  5. Confirm you have an EDR solution deployed across all endpoints.
  6. Identify who you would call (legal, cyber insurance, IT, executive leadership) if ransomware hit tonight — and write it down.

Final Thought: Ransomware Is a Business Problem, Not Just an IT Problem

The organizations that handle ransomware best are the ones where the executive leadership team has engaged with the risk before an incident occurs. That means understanding your backup posture, your cyber insurance coverage, your legal notification obligations, and your incident response plan — not as technical documents, but as business continuity tools.

Ransomware is no longer a question of if. For most businesses, it's a question of when — and how well-prepared you are when it happens. The good news is that the controls that matter most are not exotic or prohibitively expensive. They require planning, discipline, and the right expertise.
